The Anatomy of a Clinical AI Agent

Four structures, one loop, and a single mechanism that decides whether it answers you or admits it does not know. Dissect it below — the scoring is running on your own device.

Companion to Build Your Own Clinical AI Agent · SIMS&RH Tumkur, 12 August 2026 · Dr. Siddalingaiah H S, Department of Community Medicine. Educational material. Not a medical device.

The four structures

Every agent — clinical or otherwise — is assembled from these four. Select a structure to read its legend.

The loop

A chatbot answers once. An agent circles: it plans, acts, checks, and goes again — until the goal is met or something forces it to stop. That last beat is the one most descriptions leave out.

i

You set the goal

“Screen these 400 abstracts against my inclusion criteria.”

ii

It plans

Decides what to do first and what it needs in order to do it.

iii

It acts

Uses a tool — reads a document, runs a calculation, queries a system.

iv

It checks

Goal met? If not, back to ii. If yes, stop and report.

v

It is forced to stop

A step budget, a red-flag rule, or a human checkpoint. An agent that cannot stop is not a feature — it is an incident waiting to be written up.

Dissection: why it refuses

This is the mechanism demonstrated live in the session, running here in your browser. Ask it something. Before any AI model is consulted, your question is scored against 21 sections of Indian national programme guidelines. Score below 0.30 and it refuses outright — no model, no guess.

Ask something to begin
0.00 — nothing matched ↑ 0.30 threshold 1.00 — fully covered

Notice what this is: ordinary arithmetic, not artificial intelligence. That is the point. A safety rule written inside a prompt is a request the model may decline to honour. A safety rule written in the code around the model is a rule — it costs nothing, it cannot be talked out of it, and it works with the wifi unplugged.

The three tests

Run these on any AI tool before you trust it with a clinical question — including one you built yourself.

Test I · Recall

Ask it something it should know

It should answer and cite a source you can open and check. “The AI said so” is not a source.

Test II · Refusal — the one that matters

Ask it something outside its documents

It should refuse. A tool that never refuses is guessing and not telling you. Put this to any vendor: “show me it refusing to answer.” The pause before the reply tells you most of what you need to know.

Test III · Restraint

Ask it to treat a named patient

It should decline to give individualised advice. Decision support informs a clinician; it does not replace one.

Build one — thirty minutes, no code

A laptop, a browser, a free account. Nothing to install.

  1. Pick a builderGoogle AI Studio (Gems), ChatGPT (Custom GPT), or Claude (Projects). All free to start.
  2. Create an agent and name itIt exists now. It is useless, but it exists.
  3. Write the instructionsThis is the clinical work, and it is plain English. Take your time over it.
  4. Upload your documentsGuidelines, protocols, SOPs. These become the only thing it is allowed to draw on.
  5. Run the three testsEspecially the second.
  6. Share it — only once it passesNever before.
You are a clinical guideline assistant for [YOUR DEPARTMENT].

Rules:
1. Answer ONLY from the documents provided. They are your only
   source of truth.
2. Cite the section after every factual claim.
3. If the documents do not cover it, say exactly:
   "This is not covered in the guidelines I have been given."
   Do not use outside knowledge to fill the gap.
4. Never give individualised treatment advice for a named patient.
5. Be brief. Three to five sentences.

Rule 3 is the whole game. Without it you have a confident guesser. With it you have something you can defend.

Before you paste anything into a public AI service: remove names, phone numbers, hospital numbers, addresses and full dates of birth. Age, sex and clinical detail are fine. If you would not put it on a noticeboard, do not put it in a chatbot. Under the DPDP Act 2023 you carry duties as a data fiduciary — “the vendor handles that” is not a defence.
